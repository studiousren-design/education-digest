# AI in Education Digest — 2026-08-14

**United States — NSF/NIH grant proposals (Northwestern University study)** — A new study published in PNAS analyzed 5,700 confidential grant proposals and 131,000 publicly released awards submitted to the NSF and NIH between 2021 and 2025, finding that heavy LLM involvement correlates with proposals that are less semantically distinctive and increasingly resemble recently funded projects. LLM use in these federal funding proposals has risen sharply since 2023, tracking alongside the measured decline in idea originality.
*For educators:* If the same drafting tools that save researchers time also make their proposals converge on what already won funding, what happens to the kind of research too odd to resemble anything previously rewarded?
Source: https://www.timeshighereducation.com/news/large-language-models-reduce-originality-research-proposals

**Online higher education (asynchronous programs)** — An instructor teaching leadership and conflict-resolution courses describes using generative AI to build interactive roleplay simulations — negotiation, mediation, difficult conversations — so that asynchronous online students, who otherwise lack live partners to practice with, get the same experiential interpersonal-skills practice as in-person cohorts. The approach targets a specific gap: students in online programs could learn the theory but had no consistent way to rehearse the human skills the theory was meant to support.
*For educators:* If AI can manufacture a believable practice partner for hard conversations, which other "you had to be in the room" skills might now be teachable online?
Source: https://www.timeshighereducation.com/campus/using-ai-create-practice-opportunities-online-students-need

**United States — early childhood workforce (Teaching Strategies / Muon Global)** — Nicol Russell, chief academic officer at Teaching Strategies, and Dr. Nneka McGee, founder of Muon Global, argue that as AI reaches early-learning classrooms, the more urgent problem is whether pre-K educators are treated and supported as professionals at all — citing high turnover and inconsistent career pathways as the real constraint on quality, not the presence or absence of AI tools. Their framing shifts the early-childhood AI conversation away from adoption rates and toward whether the workforce meant to use these tools is stable enough to do so well.
*For educators:* Before asking whether pre-K teachers are using AI, is anyone asking whether they're staying in the job long enough for any tool — AI or otherwise — to matter?
Source: https://www.edsurge.com/news/what-do-the-youngest-learners-in-the-building-actually-need
