# AI in Education Digest — September 16, 2026

**[Hong Kong / Taiwan / Japan / New Zealand]** — Assessment experts are warning that AI-enabled smart glasses have dealt a "checkmate" blow to traditional exams: a Hong Kong University of Science and Technology test found AI-glasses-assisted answers scored in the top 5% of 100+ students, while a Taiwanese medical-school applicant was caught when her glasses' frame gave off heat, and a Japanese teenager was referred to prosecutors for photographing entrance-exam questions with camera-equipped glasses and sharing them with paid tutors in real time. The University of Auckland has since disciplined its own student for wearing the devices into a test, showing the problem has spread well beyond East Asia.

*For educators:* If banning a device is unenforceable the moment it looks like ordinary eyewear, what does "cheating" even mean for an exam format built on the assumption that you can see what a student is wearing?

**[United Kingdom]** — The University of Southampton has dropped its contract with plagiarism-detection giant Turnitin rather than accept proposed terms that would have let the company use students' submitted work to train its AI models. It's a rare case of a university walking away from a widely-used anti-cheating tool specifically over data-rights concerns, not cost or performance.

*For educators:* Your students' essays already trained the AI systems now used to catch them cheating — should institutions be negotiating data-training rights as hard as they negotiate licensing fees?

Source: https://www.timeshighereducation.com/news/southampton-dumps-turnitin-over-use-students-work-train-ai

**[United States — multilingual classrooms]** — Educators and Harvard researchers are piloting AI chatbots that scaffold instruction for emergent bilingual students, offering explanations in a student's home language, simplified sentence structures, and 24/7 low-stakes speaking practice that positions students' first language as an asset rather than a deficit. Researchers caution the same tools still struggle with accented or dialectal speech, risking new equity gaps if accuracy isn't addressed deliberately.

*For educators:* If an AI tool understands standard-accent English far better than it understands your students' actual accents, is it closing the language gap or just moving it somewhere less visible?
