# AI in Education Digest — September 18, 2026

**[Hong Kong]** — The Chinese University of Hong Kong's Nethersole School of Nursing partnered with edtech firm Votee AI to build an AI-driven virtual "elderly client" that social work and nursing students use to practice acceptance and commitment therapy counselling, with the model trained on clinical literature and running entirely on CUHK's own infrastructure to keep training data in-house. The tool is designed to absorb the repetitive parts of clinical rehearsal so that scarce real-world placements can be reserved for what only an actual clinic can teach.

*For educators:* If an AI "patient" can safely absorb the repetitive parts of clinical training, what should real placements be reserved for instead?

Source: https://www.timeshighereducation.com/news/can-ai-patients-improve-medical-training

**[United Kingdom / Global]** — On September 16, 42 fellows of the UK's Royal Society signed an open letter — since grown to nearly 200 signatories — urging the society to declare the pace of AI development an "emergency," pointing to models from OpenAI and Anthropic that now match top human mathematicians, including an OpenAI system that solved the Navier-Stokes problem, a question mathematicians had worked on for roughly 90 years. The letter is forcing renewed debate among academics about what's left to teach in mathematics once AI can already outperform students and many professionals at the frontier of the field.

*For educators:* If AI can already solve problems that stumped humans for 90 years, what's the pedagogical case for still teaching students to solve them by hand?

Source: https://www.timeshighereducation.com/news/royal-society-urged-declare-ai-emergency

**[United States]** — EdSurge reports that YouTube and similar platforms are increasingly flooded with quickly-produced, low-substance "AI slop" videos aimed at preschoolers and kindergartners, and early-childhood teachers worry repeated exposure could affect young children's still-developing attention spans. The piece offers teachers concrete vetting tactics, such as checking a channel's creation date against its video output and watching for jumbled or misspelled on-screen text — telltale signs of AI generation.

*For educators:* If AI content made for toddlers is increasingly hard to tell apart from real educational video, whose job is it to vet what a four-year-old watches — the platform, the parent, or the teacher?
