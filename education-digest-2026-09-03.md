# Education Digest — September 3, 2026

**[United States — K-12]** — OpenAI's new ChatGPT for Teens (launched Aug. 18) bundles a "Study Mode" that walks students through guiding questions instead of handing over answers, homework reminders that redirect shortcut-seekers back into that mode, and parent- or teen-set "Study Hours." An Oklahoma elementary principal and an Alabama 11th grader who both test-drove it agree it's genuinely useful for tutoring-starved students, but neither thinks it stops a determined student from finding a workaround to plagiarize.

*For educators:* If a chatbot can be configured to teach rather than answer, whose job is it to make sure that setting stays on — the platform's, the school's, or the family's?

**[United States — K-12]** — A high school English teacher recounts joining GenerationAI, a cohort-based professional development program built around identifying one concrete "problem of practice," which she used to find AI tools that could support English learners' writing. She started the program skeptical that a few workshops at Google's headquarters could change her teaching, and came away less an AI expert than a teacher newly willing to experiment in front of her students.

*For educators:* Would a structured, cohort-based "problem of practice" model do more for your staff's AI adoption than another one-off training session?

**[United Kingdom — Higher Education]** — The University of Manchester became the first university in the world to give Microsoft 365 Copilot to its entire community — 65,000 staff and students — pairing the rollout with a student advisory group and roughly 50 "listening sessions" to track how people actually use it. Manchester's blanket approach contrasts with Liverpool, which is running a slower, staged proof-of-concept trial instead, and critics warn that handing every student the same powerful tool at once risks normalizing uncritical use before guardrails are in place.

*For educators:* Is a fast, universal AI rollout with active monitoring safer than a cautious pilot — or does going slow just delay problems a full rollout would surface immediately?
