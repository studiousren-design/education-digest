# Education Digest — September 24, 2026

**Spain, France, Germany, Portugal & the UK (business schools)** — A new Graduate Management Admission Council report based on interviews with ten leading European business school deans finds that generative AI has made written coursework an unreliable signal of independent thinking, pushing schools back toward oral defenses and pen-and-paper exams. Nova School of Business and Economics (Portugal) has extended the time students spend defending theses, Frankfurt School of Finance and Management (Germany) has revived oral exams and final presentations, and HEC Paris (France) now pairs "machine off" assessments that test foundational knowledge with "machine on" exercises that test AI fluency.

*For educators:* If a written submission can no longer prove a student did the thinking, should grading shift from what students produce to how they can explain and defend it live?

Source: https://www.timeshighereducation.com/news/business-schools-bring-back-oral-defences-student-ai-use-rises

**United States (Virginia, K-12 leadership)** — A principal in a large Virginia school division describes how AI tools didn't mainly save time but restored something scarcer: uninterrupted attention, by handling first-pass drafts, meeting synthesis, and recurring parent and staff communications. Rather than one-off prompts, they built repeatable AI workflows for feedback, messaging, and organizing evidence, freeing mental space to be more present in classrooms and more intentional coaching teachers.

*For educators:* Is your school judging AI's value only by hours saved, when its bigger payoff might be the cognitive space it frees up for the judgment calls that actually require your full attention?

Source: https://www.edsurge.com/news/why-ai-made-me-a-better-principal

**United States (national policy conversation)** — Speaking at the Reagan Institute Summit on Education, Jobs for the Future president Maria Flynn argued that every graduating student now needs baseline AI literacy the way an earlier generation needed to learn typing, citing Ohio State's effort to embed AI across all its majors and Miami Dade College's applied AI programs as working models. She also flagged the risk of "cognitive surrender," where students learn to defer entirely to AI outputs instead of building independent judgment.

*For educators:* Where does your own use of AI in class sit on the line between building genuine fluency and quietly training students toward cognitive surrender?
