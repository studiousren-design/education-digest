# AI in Education Digest — 2026-08-06

**United States — Birmingham, Alabama** — EdSurge reports on the gap between AI policy on paper and AI policy in practice: researcher Jody Britten spent a year coding AI policy from more than 100 U.S. school districts into a five-level framework (from active promotion to outright prohibition) and found most districts cluster toward caution and are often disconnected from the state guidance meant to inform them. The piece pairs that national data with Tambra Clark's account of writing her own Birmingham district's AI governance policy from scratch, showing how one committee actually made the calls Britten's research only measures from a distance.
*For educators:* If your district's AI policy was written without much data behind it, what would you want a researcher like Britten to measure next to make the next revision less of a guess?
Source: https://www.edsurge.com/news/what-happens-when-ai-policy-meets-a-real-classroom

**United Kingdom — Edinburgh Napier University study** — Times Higher Education covers a survey of more than 6,000 UK university students finding that 32% report not using AI in their studies at all — self-described "conscientious objectors" — while among those who do use it, 67% said they would not use AI to help with an assessment if explicitly told not to (though 5.2% of habitual users admitted doing so anyway "most of the time" or "always"). The study also found stark usage gaps by gender and by native language, with non-native English speakers turning to AI daily or weekly far more often than native speakers.
*For educators:* Given that a third of students say they're opting out of AI entirely, how would you distinguish a student's genuine principled objection from one who's simply falling behind on AI literacy?
Source: https://www.timeshighereducation.com/news/majority-students-deny-using-ai-when-forbidden-study-finds

**United Kingdom — HEPI study of 96 university AI policies** — Times Higher Education reports on Higher Education Policy Institute research analyzing AI policies at 163 UK institutions with degree-awarding powers, finding that of the 96 with publicly accessible policies, most "promise support but deliver surveillance" — emphasizing critical AI literacy in their language while functioning in practice as conditional-trust systems built on declaration, evidence retention, and verification rather than trust itself. Only four policies (Durham, Stirling, Canterbury Christ Church, and Arts University Plymouth) were judged to hold up to scrutiny, with Durham's 9,000-word policy singled out for explicitly separating academic misconduct from merely "unwise or unethical" AI use.
*For educators:* Read your own institution's AI policy with Durham's distinction in mind — does it treat unwise AI use as a teaching moment, or does it default straight to a misconduct process?
Source: https://www.timeshighereducation.com/news/university-ai-policies-promise-support-deliver-surveillance
